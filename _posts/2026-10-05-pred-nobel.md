Why Is a Nobel Prize for Berry a Historical Inevitability?

Looking at the timing of Nobel Prize awards, we can discern two broad patterns:

1. Major scientific achievements that have advanced human knowledge tend to be recognized in roughly the order in which they were made.
2. An exceptionally groundbreaking discovery may break this chronological pattern and receive recognition very quickly.

These two patterns provide a useful lens through which to review the Nobel Prizes in Physics awarded over the past decade:

2016: Topology. David Thouless and Michael Kosterlitz developed the theory of the Kosterlitz-Thouless (KT) transition in 1973, explaining how a topological phase transition can occur in two-dimensional systems without conventional long-range order. Duncan Haldane subsequently revealed the importance of topology in quantum spin chains in 1983.

2017: Gravitational waves. Einstein had predicted gravitational waves a century earlier. In 2015, LIGO confirmed their existence by detecting a signal from the merger of two black holes approximately 1.3 billion light-years away. This exemplifies the second pattern: rapid recognition following a decisive breakthrough.

2018: Laser physics. Arthur Ashkin developed optical tweezers and demonstrated their ability to manipulate living biological objects in 1987. Gérard Mourou and Donna Strickland introduced chirped pulse amplification (CPA) in 1985, enabling enormous increases in achievable laser intensity, approaching ten orders of magnitude.

2019: Cosmology and exoplanets. James Peebles's work from the 1960s through the 1980s laid foundations for the Lambda-CDM (ΛCDM) cosmological model. In 1995, Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz discovered the first confirmed exoplanet orbiting a Sun-like star.

2020: Black holes. In 1965, Roger Penrose showed that black-hole formation is a robust consequence of general relativity. Independent observations by Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez, including landmark results published between 1996 and 1998, provided compelling evidence for a supermassive black hole at the center of the Milky Way.

2021: Climate and complex systems. Syukuro Manabe developed physical models of the climate, including pioneering studies in 1967 and 1975 of the effects of doubling atmospheric CO₂. Klaus Hasselmann proposed a stochastic climate model in 1976 that linked short-term weather fluctuations to long-term climate behavior. Through his work on spin glasses, Giorgio Parisi established a new framework for studying complex systems, introducing replica symmetry breaking (RSB) in 1979.

2022: Quantum entanglement. John Clauser in 1972 and Alain Aspect in 1982 experimentally demonstrated violations of Bell inequalities, providing strong evidence for the predictions of quantum mechanics. Anton Zeilinger further advanced experiments with entangled particles during the 1990s, including the realization of quantum teleportation.

2023: Attosecond physics. In 1987, Anne L'Huillier discovered that intense laser light interacting with noble gases produces a large number of high-order harmonics. In 2001, Pierre Agostini generated a train of attosecond pulses, while Ferenc Krausz generated an isolated attosecond pulse.

2024: Artificial intelligence and neural networks. In 1982, John Hopfield connected neural networks with energy-based models from statistical physics and introduced the Hopfield network. During the 1980s, Geoffrey Hinton developed Boltzmann machines using ideas from statistical physics.

2025: Macroscopic quantum tunneling. In a series of experiments and Physical Review Letters papers from 1984 to 1985, John Clarke, Michel Devoret, and John Martinis used superconducting circuits and Josephson junctions to demonstrate macroscopic quantum tunneling and quantized energy levels. Their work laid a foundation for modern superconducting qubits and quantum computers.

On this reading, apart from a few exceptional cases that follow the second pattern, the historical clock of the Nobel Prize in Physics is still hovering around 1984. Two achievements from that year stand out as worthy of Nobel recognition but have not yet received it specifically:

1. Berry's geometric phase. In 1984, Michael Berry showed that a quantum system initially in an eigenstate can acquire an additional geometric phase when the parameters of its Hamiltonian undergo an adiabatic cycle. This phase depends on the geometry of the path through parameter space. Berry's framework encompassed the Aharonov-Bohm (AB) effect and generalized the understanding of geometric phases from real-space settings to parameter space. In momentum space, Berry curvature, mathematically the curl of the Berry connection, provides a profound geometric explanation of the intrinsic anomalous Hall effect (AHE) in condensed matter.

2. The structure of spin-glass states. In 1984, Marc Mézard, Giorgio Parisi, Nicolas Sourlas, Gérard Toulouse, and Miguel Virasoro gave a clear microscopic account of the organization of low-energy states in spin glasses, revealing ultrametricity and non-self-averaging. Their work transformed the replica method from an abstract mathematical technique into a concrete picture of the structure of state space, becoming a cornerstone of the science of complex systems. Gross and Mézard subsequently studied the p-spin glass model. Mézard's work on high-dimensional energy landscapes provided a foundation for contemporary approaches to understanding generalization and training optimization in deep learning. These ideas also helped illuminate memory storage and optimization in neural networks such as the Hopfield model, among many other problems.

The Nobel Prize in Physics thus appears to lag many major experimental and theoretical advances by roughly 30 to 40 years. One possible reason is the sheer number of achievements deserving recognition: candidates effectively have to wait their turn, and those who die before receiving the prize are left with a tragic missed opportunity. Another is that experimental verification of modern physical theories can take a very long time, reflecting a growing gap between theoretical development and experimental confirmation.

From this historical perspective, this year's laureates would be expected to have made outstanding contributions between 1980 and 1990, with work that continues to have a broad, positive impact today. Taking their age and scientific influence into account, I therefore predict that Michael Berry and Yakir Aharonov have a very strong chance of winning this year's Nobel Prize in Physics.
